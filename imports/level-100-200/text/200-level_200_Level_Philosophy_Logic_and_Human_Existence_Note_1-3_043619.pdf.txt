Philosophy, Logic and Human Existence
Introduction to Philosophy
Definition, Nature, and Scope of Philosophy
Philosophy is derived from the Greek words philo (love) and sophia (wisdom), meaning "love of
wisdom." It is a discipline that involves the systematic study of fundamental questions about
existence, knowledge, values, reason, mind, and language. Philosophy is both a theoretical and
practical field, engaging in deep reflection on life's most profound questions.
Nature of Philosophy: Philosophy is characterized by its critical, systematic, and reflective
nature. It is concerned with asking and answering deep, fundamental questions about the world
and human existence. The nature of philosophy includes:
 Critical Thinking: Philosophy challenges established beliefs and promotes logical
reasoning and inquiry.
 Systematic Inquiry: It employs structured methods to explore fundamental issues.
 Universality: Philosophy deals with concepts that are relevant across different cultures
and times.
 Reflectiveness: Encourages self-examination and the questioning of assumptions.
Scope of Philosophy: Philosophy is a broad discipline that influences various aspects of human
life, including science, politics, ethics, religion, and art. Its scope covers multiple areas of human
inquiry, and it provides frameworks for analyzing complex problems and developing reasoned
solutions.
Branches of Philosophy
Philosophy is generally divided into five major branches, each dealing with different aspects of
human thought and existence:
1. Metaphysics – The study of reality, existence, and the fundamental nature of being. It
explores questions such as: What is real? What is the nature of existence? What is the
relationship between mind and matter?
2. Epistemology – The study of knowledge, its nature, sources, and limits. It investigates
questions like: What can we know? How do we acquire knowledge? What distinguishes
justified belief from opinion?
3. Ethics – The study of moral principles that govern human behavior. It examines concepts
of right and wrong, justice, virtue, and moral responsibility.
4. Logic – The study of reasoning, argumentation, and the principles of valid inference.
Logic helps distinguish between valid and fallacious reasoning.
5. Aesthetics – The study of beauty, art, and taste. It seeks to understand what makes things
aesthetically pleasing and how artistic expression impacts human experience.

Importance of Philosophy in Human Life
Philosophy plays a crucial role in shaping human thought, decision-making, and problem-solving.
1. Enhancing Critical Thinking – Philosophy trains individuals to analyze arguments,
identify biases, and think logically. This skill is essential in making well-reasoned
decisions in both personal and professional life.
2. Moral and Ethical Guidance – Ethical philosophy helps individuals and societies
establish moral values and frameworks that govern behavior.
3. Understanding Human Existence – Through existential and metaphysical inquiry,
philosophy helps people seek meaning, purpose, and a better understanding of their place
in the world.
4. Improving Communication Skills – Engaging in philosophical discourse enhances
clarity in reasoning, persuasion, and articulation of ideas.
5. Influencing Other Disciplines – Philosophy underpins many other fields, including law,
politics, science, and technology, by providing foundational principles and methods of
inquiry.
6. Promoting Social and Political Awareness – Political philosophy informs discussions
on justice, rights, governance, and the responsibilities of individuals within a society.
7. Contributing to Technological and Scientific Advancements – The philosophy of
science examines the methods, assumptions, and ethical implications of scientific
discoveries and technological innovations.
Characteristics of a Philosopher
1. Critical and Rational Inquiry – Philosophy involves logical reasoning and questioning rather
than relying on assumptions or dogma.
2. Systematic and Structured Thinking – Philosophical arguments follow structured methods of
reasoning and analysis.
3. Fundamental Questions – Philosophy addresses deep questions about existence, knowledge,
morality, reality, and human purpose.
4. Universality and Generality – Unlike specialized sciences, philosophy seeks broad,
foundational principles that apply universally.
5. Conceptual Analysis – Philosophers analyze the meanings, implications, and interconnections of
concepts.
6. Reflective and Self-Examinatory – Philosophy encourages self-awareness and critical
examination of one's beliefs and assumptions.
7. Interdisciplinary Nature – It intersects with other disciplines such as science, religion, politics,
and ethics.
8. Search for Wisdom – Philosophy is not just about acquiring knowledge but seeking wisdom in
understanding life and the universe.
9. Dialectical Method – Philosophical discussions often involve argumentation, counterarguments,
and refinement of ideas through dialogue.
10. Normative and Evaluative – Philosophy not only describes reality but also prescribes values and
ethical standards.

2. The Origins and History of Philosophy
 Early beginnings: African, Eastern, and Western traditions
Philosophy originated as a product of man’s gift of rationality which aids him in his interaction
with nature, the environment and human experiences (Molokwu, 2009). Though traces of the
origin always start with the western traditions, particularly, the Greeks, its presence in Africa and
the Eastern part of the world cannot be overlooked. The early philosophical traditions of Africa,
the East (including China and India), and the West (particularly Greece) reveal diverse
philosophical thoughts that shaped human intellectual history.
In Africa, Egyptian civilization which is one of the oldest in human history, played a role in the
development of early philosophical thought as Egyptian philosophy which was mixed with
mythology, ethics, and governance, emphasised the concept of Ma'at—order, justice, and balance
(Ebo, 2018). Philosophers such as Ptahhotep, who authored "The Maxims of Ptahhotep,"
provided ethical guidance on leadership, humility, and social harmony. Moreover, recent
scholarship suggests that early Greek philosophers, including Thales and Pythagoras, were
influenced by Egyptian thought as the Greeks borrowed heavily from Egyptian metaphysics and
mathematical principles (Anakwue, 2018). Beyond Egypt, African philosophy thrived in oral
traditions and the wisdom of sages. The Akan, Yoruba, and Igbo peoples had sophisticated
systems of thought, often transmitted through proverbs, folklore, and storytelling (Kanu, 2013).
In Eastern part of the world, the Indian philosophical tradition dates back to the Vedas (c. 1500
BCE), which contain hymns and metaphysical speculations about the nature of existence and the
universe. The Upanishads (800–200 BCE) further developed concepts such as Brahman (ultimate
reality) and Atman (self), forming the basis of Vedantic thought (Osborne, 1992). According to
Osborne (1992), six major philosophical schools—Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga,
Mimamsa, and Vedanta—offered different approaches to epistemology, logic, and ethics.
Nonetheless, the emergence of Buddhism and Jainism in the 6th century BCE introduced new
perspectives on suffering, karma, and liberation. Furthermore, in China, early philosophical
thought was shaped by Confucianism, Daoism, and Legalism. Confucius (551–479 BCE)
focused on moral virtue, social harmony, and the role of education in personal development. His
Analects remain central to Chinese thought (Osborne, 1992). Daoism, attributed to Laozi, offered
a contrasting view that valued natural spontaneity and harmony with the Dao (Way). Meanwhile,
Legalism, championed by Han Feizi, focused on strict laws and governance as essential for
societal order.
Western philosophy is said to have started with wonder and curiosity about the nature of the
universe (Molokwu, 2009). It is often traced to the Pre-Socratic thinkers of Ionia, such as Thales,
Anaximander, and Heraclitus, who sought natural explanations for the cosmos, moving away
from mythological narratives (Ebo, 2018). The achievements of these philosophers will be
discussed later.

 Key philosophical movements: Ancient, Medieval, Modern, and
Contemporary
Philosophy, as a discipline, has undergone transformations over centuries and has been shaped
by cultural, political, and scientific developments. Therefore, this sub-section looks into four key
philosophical movements: Ancient, Medieval, Modern, and Contemporary philosophy, as each
period contributed unique perspectives and helped in shaping the evolution of thought and
influencing modern philosophical inquiries.
I. Ancient Period
Ancient philosophy which emerged in the 6th century BCE, focused on understanding the nature
of reality, ethics, and knowledge. According to Molokwu (2009), this period can be divided into
two: The pre-Socratic and Socratic periods.
The pre-Socratic period focused more on issues related to cosmology—the universe and its
constitutive elements and the philosophers of the period were known as members of the Milesian
school (Molokwu, 2009). Some of the philosophers of this period include philosophers such as
Thales, Anaximander, and Heraclitus, who initiated inquiries into metaphysics and cosmology
(Sokal, Oden, Rosenberg, & Digiovanni, 1997).
Thales, the first member of this school, contended that the source or origin of the universe was
water as he believed that all things are made from it. Thales’ contributions spanned various fields,
including philosophy, science, mathematics, and astronomy. Despite the scarcity of primary
sources, much of what is known about Thales comes from later philosophers, particularly
Aristotle, who credited him as the first philosopher to propose a naturalistic explanation for the
cosmos (Cantor, 2022).
Thales’ philosophical approach was a radical departure from traditional mythological narratives.
He proposed that water was the underlying principle (arche) of all matter, suggesting that the
world’s complexity could be reduced to a single, fundamental substance. This concept of
material monism introduced a new method of inquiry based on natural causes rather than
supernatural explanations. Kartal (2023) discusses how Aristotle interpreted Thales’ theory as an
early attempt at material monism, a concept that would later influence the development of
metaphysics. While Aristotle critiqued Thales’ idea for its simplicity, he acknowledged its
importance in initiating the tradition of seeking naturalistic explanations for natural phenomena.
One of Thales’ most significant contributions was his pioneering approach to understanding
natural events through observation and reason. He is credited with predicting a solar eclipse
which demonstrates his application of empirical methods to astronomy (Molinari, 2022). This
achievement not only showcased his knowledge of celestial patterns but also illustrated his
departure from viewing such phenomena as governed by capricious gods. According to Rajiyung
(2024), Thales’ work marked the beginning of the shift from mythos (myth) to logos (reason), a
critical transformation that laid the groundwork for scientific inquiry and the philosophical
tradition of rational analysis.
Thales’ influence extended beyond natural philosophy into mathematics. He is attributed with
several geometric principles, including what is now known as Thales’ Theorem, which states that
if a triangle is inscribed in a circle with one side as the diameter, then the triangle is a right

triangle. Kvasz (2020) argues that Thales’ approach to geometry reflected a cognitive unity
between his philosophical and mathematical thinking. His use of deductive reasoning in
mathematics parallels the methodological rigor he applied to natural philosophy.
After Thales, Anaximander, an evolutionist, came up as the second member of this school. He
stated that all things came as a product of the activity of an element whose nature is infinite,
eternal and ageless (Molokwu, 2009). As a member of the Milesian school, alongside Thales and
Anaximenes, Anaximander sought natural explanations for the origin and structure of the cosmos,
departing from the mythological frameworks that dominated ancient Greek culture. His ideas
introduced the concept of the apeiron (the “boundless” or “infinite”), a revolutionary notion that
not only influenced subsequent philosophers but also laid foundational principles for scientific
inquiry and metaphysical speculation (Stella & Divino, 2023).
Anaximander’s concept of the apeiron is one of his most enduring philosophical contributions.
Unlike his predecessor Thales, who posited water as the primary substance of all things,
Anaximander proposed that the cosmos originated from the apeiron, an indefinite and boundless
principle that transcends conventional elements like water, air, or fire (López, 2020). The
apeiron was not a specific material substance but rather an eternal and ageless source from
which all things emerge and into which they return. This cyclical process of generation and
destruction reflects a form of natural justice (dike), where the elements atone for their excesses
over time, maintaining balance in the universe (Griffith, 2022). By introducing the apeiron,
Anaximander advanced a metaphysical idea that suggested the world is governed by abstract
principles rather than capricious deities, which is a shift towards rationalism and naturalism in
Greek philosophy.
Anaximander also made substantial contributions to cosmology. He was the first known
philosopher to conceptualise the universe with a structured, mechanical order. According to
Naddaf (2019), Anaximander proposed that the Earth was a cylindrical shape, suspended freely
at the center of the cosmos, not supported by any physical structure. This idea broke away from
earlier mythological views that imagined the Earth resting on a physical foundation, such as the
back of a giant creature or a world-pillar. Anaximander’s assertion that the Earth remained in its
place due to its equidistant position from all points of the celestial sphere introduced a
rudimentary concept of equilibrium.
Furthermore, Anaximander’s work in astronomy and meteorology revealed his empirical
approach to natural phenomena. He is credited with creating one of the earliest maps of the
known world and contributing to the understanding of celestial bodies’ movements (Zhu, 2024).
His theories on the formation of the Earth and the heavens were not only speculative but also
grounded in observable phenomena, as seen in his explanation of natural events like rain, wind,
and the changing seasons. Rahman (2023) highlights Anaximander’s use of dialectical reasoning,
particularly his exploration of opposites—such as hot and cold, wet and dry—as fundamental
forces driving natural change. This dialectical method influenced later philosophical traditions,
including Heraclitus’ doctrine of change and the development of dialectics by Plato and Aristotle.

Anaximander’s philosophical ideas also extended into the realm of biology and the natural
sciences. He proposed an early theory of evolution, suggesting that life originated in the moisture
of the Earth and that human beings evolved from fish-like creatures (Salles, 2024). This proto-
evolutionary theory demonstrated his ability to apply naturalistic explanations to biological
processes. His ideas on natural processes emphasised the importance of observation and rational
analysis.
Anaximenes, the third of the Milesian school, differed in opined from both Thales and
Anaximander as he was concerned with the nature of cosmic matter or primary element (Ozuzu,
2005). Anaximenes proposed that air was the fundamental substance from which all things
originate. Unlike his predecessor Anaximander, who posited the apeiron (the “boundless”) as the
ultimate principle, Anaximenes chose a more tangible and observable element. He introduced the
theory of condensation and rarefaction (Molokwu, 2009). He argued that air could transform into
various forms of matter through processes of condensation and rarefaction. According to Hobza
(2020), Anaximenes suggested that when air condenses, it becomes visible and material,
transforming into water, then earth, and finally stones. Conversely, when air rarefies, it becomes
fire. This theory provided an early model of physical transformation based on natural processes,
which shows Anaximenes’ pioneering approach to explaining the physical world through
empirical observation and rational deduction.
Anaximenes’ understanding of air extended beyond its material aspect to a more metaphysical
interpretation. He viewed air as a life-giving force, drawing an analogy between the macrocosm
of the universe and the microcosm of human life. As air sustains human life through breathing,
so too does it sustain the universe. Deng (2023) points out that this perspective infused
Anaximenes’ philosophy with a quasi-spiritual dimension, as air was not only a physical
substance but also a source of vitality and motion.
Anaximenes’ contributions to cosmology were also significant as he envisioned a flat Earth
suspended in air, with celestial bodies such as the sun, moon, and stars floating above it
(Kočandrle, 2019). He proposed that these heavenly bodies were ignited by the movement of air
and that their rising and setting were optical illusions caused by their rotation around the Earth.
This meteorological approach to cosmology differed from modern scientific understandings but
represented a critical step toward naturalistic explanations of celestial phenomena. By attributing
cosmic events to observable natural processes, Anaximenes laid the groundwork for later
astronomical theories and challenged the notion that such events were governed by the whims of
gods.
Deng (2023) draws a comparison between Anaximenes’ concept of air as a fundamental
principle and the Chinese philosophy of Qi as described in the Wuxing (Five Elements)
framework. Both systems propose a dynamic, process-oriented understanding of the natural
world, where a basic substance transforms into different forms through specific mechanisms.
Such cross-cultural analyses underscore the universality of early human attempts to understand
the natural world through elemental theories, suggesting that Anaximenes’ thought was part of a
broader pattern of philosophical inquiry into the nature of existence.

Anaximenes’ methodology also reflects an evolution in scientific thinking. His emphasis on
natural processes like condensation and rarefaction can be seen as an early form of empirical
science, where hypotheses about natural phenomena were grounded in observable processes
(Hobza, 2020). While Anaximenes lacked the experimental rigor of modern science, his
approach contributed to the development of the scientific method by promoting the idea that
natural laws governed the behavior of matter. His influence extended to later philosophers,
including Heraclitus, who developed the concept of perpetual change, and Empedocles, who
proposed a more developed theory of the four classical elements.
Pythagoras was also an Ionia but migrated to kroton in southern Italy. He formed a movement
known as the pythagorean school (Ozuzu, 2005). Pythagoras was a genius at calculation, the
shapes of symbols and he calculated the triangle. He also believed in reincarnation and claimed
that the soul was divine and undergoes purification through life, death, and rebirth to attain
perfection (Molokwu, 2009). Despite the scarcity of direct writings attributed to Pythagoras, his
legacy is preserved through the works of later philosophers such as Plato, Aristotle, and
Iamblichus (Moore, 2019).
Central to Pythagorean philosophy is the concept that numbers constitute the fundamental
essence of reality. Pythagoras and his followers believed that “all things are numbers,”
suggesting that the universe’s structure and harmony could be understood through mathematical
relationships (Amangeldiyev & Aldanazarova, 2020). This perspective laid the groundwork for
the metaphysics of numbers, where numbers were not merely tools for counting but intrinsic
elements of the cosmos with mystical and ontological significance. The Pythagoreans proposed
that the principles of mathematics could explain natural phenomena, with particular emphasis on
the ratios found in musical harmony. Konovalova (2021) reveals how Pythagoras’ discovery of
the harmonic intervals in music—such as the octave, fifth, and fourth—demonstrated the deep
connection between numerical ratios and the physical world, leading to the concept of the
“harmony of the spheres.” This idea proposed that celestial bodies produced a form of cosmic
music through their movements, reflecting a universal order governed by mathematical laws.
Pythagoras’ contributions to mathematics extend beyond metaphysics. He is best known for the
Pythagorean theorem, which establishes the relationship between the sides of a right-angled
triangle. While this theorem was likely known to earlier cultures, Pythagoras is credited with
providing the first formal proof, a hallmark of Greek mathematical rigor (Erovenko &
Michailova, 2022). His approach to mathematics emphasised logical deduction and proof,
influencing the development of geometry and laying the foundations for Euclidean geometry.
Chiotis (2020) opines that Pythagorean mathematical principles influenced ancient architecture,
including the design of the Polycratean temple of Heraion in Samos. The use of Pythagorean
triples in temple layouts also illustrates how Pythagorean mathematics was not only theoretical
but also applied in practical contexts.
The Pythagorean school also ventured into metaphysical and religious domains. Pythagoras
introduced the doctrine of metempsychosis, or the transmigration of souls, which posits that the
soul is immortal and undergoes a cycle of rebirths (John & Akpan, 2021). This belief, influenced
by Orphic traditions, emphasises the ethical dimensions of Pythagoreanism, which advocates for

a life of purity and self-discipline to achieve spiritual liberation. De Campos (2022) opines that
Pythagoras’ unwritten teachings, preserved through oral tradition and interpreted by later
Neoplatonists like Hermias of Alexandria, contributed to a mystical strand of Greek philosophy
that valued esoteric knowledge and the inner transformation of the soul. This integration of
philosophy with religious practice positioned Pythagoras as not merely a mathematician or
scientist but as a spiritual guide whose teachings aimed at the cultivation of both mind and spirit.
Pythagorean philosophy also extended into the realm of political thought. According to Brovkin
(2024), the early Pythagoreans promoted political stability, moderation, and the integration of
philosophical principles into public life. Their community in Croton, Southern Italy, functioned
as a model of philosophical governance, emphasising equality, justice, and communal living.
However, Pythagorean influence in politics was not without controversy. The secretive nature of
their society and their elitist inclinations led to political conflicts, resulting in the persecution of
Pythagoreans and the dispersal of their community. Despite this setback, the political ideals of
Pythagoras influenced later thinkers, including Plato, whose Republic echoes Pythagorean
notions of philosopher-kings and the role of education in achieving a just society.
Pythagoras’ influence is also evident in his impact on music theory and cultural works.
Konovalova (2021) explains that Pythagoras’ insights into the mathematical basis of musical
harmony contributed to the mathematisation of music, transforming it from a mystical art into a
disciplined science. This approach laid the groundwork for Western music theory and reinforced
the idea that beauty and harmony in art are reflections of deeper mathematical and cosmic orders.
Moreover, Danesi (2020) emphasises Pythagoras’ enduring legacy in the intellectual traditions of
science and philosophy, arguing that his integration of mathematics, music, and metaphysics
established a model of interdisciplinary inquiry that continues to inspire contemporary thinkers.
Another influential philosopher of this period was Heraclitus, who argued that nothing is
permanent in this world except for change (Molokwu, 2009). Heraclitus also added that in spite
the fact that there is perpetual change in the world, there is also perpetual conflict and strife
(Ozuzu, 2005). Heraclitus of Ephesus (c. 535–475 BCE) was known for his doctrine of flux and
his enigmatic aphorisms. His philosophy, characterised by the idea of constant change and the
unity of opposites, contributed significantly to the development of Western philosophy,
particularly through his concept of the logos (reason or word). Heraclitus’ work, primarily
surviving in fragments, presents a worldview where stability is an illusion, and change is the
only constant (Desta, 2023).
Heraclitus’ doctrine of flux is famously encapsulated in his statement that one cannot step into
the same river twice, which reveals his belief that everything is in a state of continuous
transformation. According to Hui (2019), this idea contrasts sharply with later philosophical
systems, such as Plato’s theory of eternal Forms, which emphasise stability and permanence.
Heraclitus argued that all things are in a perpetual state of becoming, driven by an underlying
order he called logos. This logos is not only a principle of order but also a rational structure that
governs the cosmos, suggesting that while things change, they do so according to a rational
pattern (Hui, 2019).

The concept of logos plays a central role in Heraclitus’ philosophy. He proposed that the logos is
an objective truth accessible through reason, distinguishing it from the mythical explanations of
the world offered by poets like Homer and Hesiod (Desta, 2023). Halapsis (2020) emphasises
that Heraclitus’ logos bridges the gap between mythos and logos as it represents a shift from
mystical explanations to rational thought.
Heraclitus is also known for his principle of the unity of opposites. He believed that opposites
are not mutually exclusive but interdependent. For example, he argued that life and death, day
and night, and war and peace are interconnected, and one cannot exist without the other (De
Campos, 2022). This principle led to his famous assertion that “war is the father of all things,”
suggesting that conflict and tension drive the creation and destruction that sustain the cosmos
(Lopes, 2021). Heraclitus’ exploration of opposites laid the groundwork for dialectical thinking,
later developed by philosophers such as Hegel and Nietzsche.
Heraclitus’ philosophy of fire as the fundamental element of the cosmos is another key aspect of
his thought. He viewed fire not only as a physical substance but as a metaphor for change and
transformation. Ignatenko (2020) explains that for Heraclitus, fire represented the ever-present
process of creation and destruction, mirroring the perpetual flux of the universe.
Heraclitus’ enigmatic style and use of paradox have also contributed to his enduring legacy. His
writings are often described as obscure, using metaphor, symbolism, and aphoristic statements
that resist straightforward interpretation (Jeck, 2019).
Parmenides from Elea, was the first to focus on the difference between the senses and reason
(Molokwu, 2009). According to Ozuzu (2005), the philosophy of Parmenides is contained in his
poem title ‘On Nature,’ in which he explained two things, the way of truth and way of opinion.
According to Parmenides, the first is the way of reason while the second is the of the way of the
senses (Ozuzu, 2005). Central to Parmenides’ philosophy is the assertion that “what is, is” and
“what is not, is not,” encapsulating his principle of the immutability and unity of being. He
argued that being is ungenerated, indestructible, indivisible, and unchanging. According to Meza
(2020), Parmenides’ monism posits that reality is a singular, continuous entity, and any
perception of change or plurality is illusory. This view directly opposed earlier thinkers like
Heraclitus, who emphasised the constant flux of the universe. Parmenides’ rejection of change
and multiplicity led to a stark dichotomy between the world of appearances and the world of true
reality, influencing later philosophers such as Plato and Aristotle.
The method by which Parmenides arrived at his conclusions involves rigorous logical reasoning,
which some scholars consider an early form of deductive logic. His poem introduces the “Way of
Truth,” where he outlines the characteristics of being, and contrasts it with the “Way of Opinion,”
which deals with the deceptive nature of sensory experience. Sorensen (2022) highlights that
Parmenides’ approach to truth is not empirical but rational, relying on the intellect to access an
eternal and unchanging reality. This rationalist perspective marked a critical shift from
mythological explanations of the cosmos to a more systematic and philosophical approach to
knowledge.

Parmenides’ influence on the development of logic is also noteworthy. Çetin (2022) argues that
Parmenides’ work prefigures several principles of classical logic, including the law of non-
contradiction, the principle of identity, and the law of the excluded middle. These principles,
though not formally articulated until Aristotle, are implicit in Parmenides’ arguments against the
possibility of non-being and change. His use of logical consistency to support his ontological
claims set a precedent for the philosophical tradition of constructing metaphysical theories
through deductive reasoning.
Despite Parmenides’ emphasis on the unity of being, his poem also addresses cosmological ideas
by creating an apparent contradiction between his metaphysical monism and his descriptions of
the physical world. Conte (2023) discusses this paradox, noting that while Parmenides denies the
reality of becoming and change in the “Way of Truth,” he provides a cosmological account in the
“Way of Opinion” that includes elements of traditional Greek cosmology, such as the roles of
light and night. Scholars have debated whether this cosmological section represents a concession
to popular belief or a didactic tool to illustrate the errors of sensory perception. This dual strategy,
as suggested by Thanassas (2020), might reflect a deeper methodological approach, where
Parmenides juxtaposes truth with illusion to reinforce his argument about the unreliability of the
senses.
There were also the Atomists and Sophists. Atomists like Leucipus and Democritus believed that
everything is composed of atoms which are physically indivisible and indestructible while
Sophists were teachers who employed the deductive method often beginning with particular
cases and ending with general principle (Molokwu, 2009).
The Socratic period is the second period of the ancient period in the historical development of
philosophy. Socrates did not write anything (Molokwu, 2009); however, Bonacchi, Witte, &
Altaweel (2024) reveal that Socrates introduced ethical philosophy which emphasised rational
inquiry and dialectics and his student, Plato, expanded on these ideas, advocating for idealism
and establishing the Academy. According to Molokwu (2009), Socrates compelled philosophy to
consider lifestyle, issues of existence and morals. Thus, one of Socrates’ most significant
contributions to philosophy is his development of the Socratic method, a form of dialectical
questioning designed to stimulate critical thinking and expose contradictions in thought. This
method involves asking a series of probing questions to help individuals examine their beliefs
and arrive at a deeper understanding of truth. According to Avdić (2021), this approach marked a
critical transition from pre-Socratic natural philosophy, which focused on explaining the physical
world, to a new focus on human life, ethics, and the pursuit of virtue. Socrates believed that
through dialogue and questioning, individuals could achieve self-knowledge, which he
considered the highest form of wisdom. His famous assertion, “I know that I do not know,”
highlights his commitment to intellectual humility and the endless pursuit of knowledge (Avdić,
2021).
Socrates’ philosophy was not only methodological but also ethical. He is often credited as the
first philosopher to place ethics at the center of philosophical inquiry. Wolfsdorf (2020) argues
that Socrates’ ethical philosophy was revolutionary in its emph asis on the idea that virtue is
knowledge. According to this view, wrongdoing is a result of ignorance, and those who truly

--- page 11 ---
understand what is good will naturally do good. This perspective introduced a rational basis for
ethics, suggesting that moral excellence could be taught and learned through education and
reflection. Socrates’ ethical views also laid the groundwork for later philosophical traditions,
including stoicism and Christian ethics, which similarly emphasise the importance of wisdom
and the cultivation of the soul.
The life and death of Socrates serve as a powerful testament to his philosophical principles. In
399 BCE, he was tried and sentenced to death by the Athenian state on charges of impiety and
corrupting the youth. His trial and defense, as recorded in Plato’s Apology, show his unwavering
commitment to his philosophical mission. Jiao (2023) notes that Socrates used his trial as an
opportunity to educate the public about the importance of virtue and the examined life. Rather
than plead for his life, he maintained that a true philosopher must not fear death but instead
remain dedicated to the pursuit of truth and justice. His acceptance of the death penalty rather
than compromising his beliefs solidified his status as a martyr for philosophy, embodying the
ideal of living according to one’s principles regardless of the consequences.
Socrates’ impact on subsequent philosophy cannot be overstated. Taylor (2019) emphasises that
Socrates’ influence on Plato was particularly significant as Plato’s portrayal of Socrates not only
preserved his teachings but also transformed them into a robust philosophical framework that
underpinned the development of Western philosophy. Through Plato’s dialogues, Socratic ideas
influenced a wide array of philosophical schools, including the Academy founded by Plato,
Aristotelian philosophy, and later, the Stoics, who adopted Socratic ethics as a core component
of their doctrine. Socrates’ ideas also reached into medieval thought through the revival of
Platonic philosophy during the Renaissance.
Plato (c. 427–347 BCE), a student who was greatly influence by his teacher, Socrates,
contributed immensely to the development of philosophy. To him, man is made of the soul
(reason) and the body (Molokwu, 2009). Plato introduced the concept of Forms (or Ideas),
proposing that the material world is a mere shadow of a higher, unchanging reality of abstract,
perfect entities. According to Nicolae Sfetcu (2022), Plato distinguished between perceptible but
unintelligible reality (empirical knowledge) and imperceptible but intelligible reality (rational
knowledge) by drawing influence from Pythagoras and mathematics. Plato emphasised the role
of reason in acquiring knowledge. He argued that true knowledge (episteme) comes from rational
insight rather than sensory experience (doxa or mere opinion). His dialectical method, based on
structured argumentation, remains central to philosophical inquiry (Herrmann, 2007). Plato’s
ethical theory was rooted in his concept of the tripartite soul, consisting of reason, spirit, and
appetite. He also argued that Justice emerges when these elements function harmoniously. Plato
also made contributions to mathematical philosophy and its application in natural sciences. Wu
Wen (2013) explains that he integrated mathematical reasoning into his metaphysical and
scientific outlook, influencing fields such as astronomy. Plato founded the Academy in Athens,
one of the first higher-learning institutions in the Western world (Ahemad & Shah, 2016).
Aristotle, Plato’s student, advanced empiricism by laying the groundwork for scientific
methodology. His works in ethics, politics, and metaphysics influenced various disciplines
(Narasimhan et al., 2019). Molokwu (2009) reveals that he founded a school called Lyceum and

--- page 12 ---
was known to have developed formal logic which deals with the form that reasoning takes. One
of Aristotle’s significant contributions to philosophy is his work in metaphysics, where he
explored the nature of being and reality. His seminal text, Metaphysics, introduces the concept of
‘substance’ as the primary category of being. Aristotle argued that substance is composed of both
matter and form, where matter is the potentiality and form is the actuality (Meister, 2022). He
proposed that the ultimate reality is not separate from the physical world, as Plato suggested, but
is present within tangible objects through their forms. Aristotle’s doctrine of the “four causes”
(material, formal, efficient, and final) provided a comprehensive framework for explaining
change and existence in the natural world. His notion of the “unmoved mover” as the first cause
of all motion influenced theological and philosophical discourse for centuries, particularly in
medieval scholasticism (Jeffrey, 2021).
Aristotle’s contributions to logic are foundational to Western philosophy. His Organon
introduced formal logic and syllogistic reasoning, establishing a method of deductive reasoning
that dominated philosophical thought until the modern era (Castelli, 2020). Aristotle’s
categorisation of logical propositions and his development of the principles of non-contradiction
and the excluded middle became critical tools for evaluating philosophical arguments and
scientific hypotheses. Aristotle categorised propositions into four types: universal affirmative,
universal negative, particular affirmative, and particular negative. Taysina (2023) emphasises
that Aristotle’s methodological arsenal, particularly his use of categories, provided a structured
approach to philosophical inquiry that influenced not only ancient thought but also modern
philosophy through figures like Immanuel Kant.
In ethics, Aristotle is best known for his concept of eudaimonia, often translated as “happiness”
or “flourishing.” His ‘Nicomachean Ethics’ argues that the highest human good is living a life of
rational activity in accordance with virtue (Melnikov, 2023). Aristotle distinguished between
intellectual virtues, which involve reasoning, and moral virtues, which arise from habit. He
introduced the “Golden Mean,” a doctrine that virtue lies between the extremes of excess and
deficiency. For example, courage is a mean between recklessness and cowardice. Aristotle’s
ethical theory is a form of virtue ethics, which contrasts with consequentialist and deontological
ethical frameworks developed later in Western philosophy. His ideas on ethics were not only
theoretical but also practical, influencing political philosophy and education.
Aristotle’s political philosophy is articulated in Politics, where he explores the role of the polis
(city-state) in achieving the good life. He believed that humans are “political animals” whose
fulfillment is closely tied to their participation in civic life (Ishino, 2022). Aristotle analysed
different forms of government, including monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy and proposed a
mixed regime as the most stable and just form of governance. He emphasised the importance of
the middle class in maintaining political stability and critiqued both extreme democracy and
oligarchy for their potential to lead to tyranny. Unlike Plato, who envisioned a philosopher-king
ruling the ideal state, Aristotle advocated for a more pragmatic approach that considered
historical and cultural contexts (Trang & Quỳnh, 2021).

Medieval philosophy, spanning roughly from the 5th to the 15th century, helped in preserving and
transforming classical philosophical traditions. This period was influenced by Greek, Roman,
Christian, Jewish, and Islamic thought and sought to reconcile faith with reason, laying the
foundation for modern philosophy. Following the fall of the Western Roman Empire,
philosophical activity shifted towards preserving ancient knowledge and integrating it with
Christian theology. Thus, medieval philosophy bridged classical philosophy with theological
doctrines. Augustine’s works integrated Platonic thought into Christianity (Cesaretti, et al., 2016).
One notable philosopher of this period was St. Augustine (354–430). Influenced by
Neoplatonism, he laid emphasises on the role of divine illumination in knowledge (Marenbon,
2006).
Boethius (477–524) translated and commented on Aristotle’s logic, bridging classical and
medieval thought (György, 2013). The monastic tradition in Europe helped preserve and
transmit classical works.
The Islamic and Jewish influence (9th–12th Century) was key to the development of philosophy
as Islamic and Jewish scholars helped in transmitting and interpreting classical philosophy.
Scholars like Avicenna (Ibn Sina) (980–1037) synthesised Aristotelianism and Neoplatonism,
contributing to metaphysics and logic (Inglis, 2002).
Averroes (Ibn Rushd) (1126–1198) emphasised on reason and provided detailed commentaries
on Aristotle which influenced Latin Scholasticism (König-Pralong, 2009).
Maimonides (1138–1204) integrated Jewish theology with Aristotelian philosophy which
impacted Christian thought.
Furthermore, in 12th–14th Century, there was the translation movement in Islamic Spain which
reintroduced Aristotle’s works to Christian Europe, leading to Scholasticism. This era of
scholasticism had scholars like St. Anselm (1033–1109) who developed the ontological
argument for God’s existence (Deely, 2010).
Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) merged Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology,
emphasising natural reason and divine revelation (Schmiga & Porro, 2013). He synthesised
advocated for natural law and rational theology. His work remains a cornerstone of Catholic
philosophy (Musielak, Kubicka, Woźniak, Jóźwiak, & Liu, 2021). John Duns Scotus (1266–
1308) and William of Ockham (1287–1347) challenged Aquinas, introducing Nominalism,
which questioned the reality of universals (Pomplun, 2016).
In the late Medieval period (14th–15th Century) William of Ockham proposed the Ockham’s
Razor, which emphasised simplicity in philosophical reasoning, anticipating scientific
empiricism (Risti & Gorgiev, 2006). Also, the decline of Scholasticism and the rise of
Renaissance humanism shifted the focus from theological concerns to human-centered inquiry.
The revival of Platonic and Aristotelian traditions in the Renaissance set the stage for modern
philosophy (Gilson, 2019).

II. Modern Philosophy
Modern philosophy (17th-19th century) shifted towards individual reason and empirical
observation. It marked a transition from medieval scholasticism to new ways of understanding
reality, knowledge, and human existence. During this period, the Age of Reason, scientific
advancements, and political revolutions greatly influenced philosophical thought, leading to
Rationalism, Empiricism, Idealism, Existentialism, and Analytic Philosophy. Descartes’ cogito
argument laid the foundation for rationalism, while Locke and Hume developed empiricism
(Bašić, Kružić, Jerković, Anđelinović, & Anđelinović, 2017).
A. The Early Modern Period (17th–18th Century)
In the early modern period, there was focus on Rationalism which emphasised reason as the
source of knowledge. Some scholars of rationalism this period includes:
 René Descartes (1596–1650) introduced methodological skepticism and the
famous dictum “Cogito, ergo sum” (I think, therefore I am) (Shapiro, 2016).
 Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) and Gottfried Leibniz (1646–1716) developed
monist and pluralist metaphysical systems.
B. Empiricism in this period emphasised experience and sensory perception as sources of
knowledge.
 John Locke (1632–1704) argued for the mind as a tabula rasa (blank slate).
 George Berkeley (1685–1753) and David Hume (1711–1776) critiqued
materialism and introduced skepticism (Mercer & O’Neill, 2005).
 Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) synthesised rationalism and empiricism in his
“Critique of Pure Reason,” arguing that knowledge is shaped by both sensory
experience and innate structures of reason (Corrias, 2019). He introduced the
concept of synthetic a priori knowledge (Eraslan, 2014).
C. German Idealism and the 19th Century
German idealism scholars in the 19th century includes:
 Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831), who developed dialectical
idealism, where reality unfolds through contradictions and resolutions (Bunge,
2014).
 Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) introduced pessimism, seeing life as driven
by irrational “Will”.
 Karl Marx (1818–1883) applied dialectics to history, proposing materialism and
class struggle as central forces shaping society [(Liao, 2000).
D. Existentialism and Phenomenology (19th–20th Century)
 Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) and Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) rejected
absolute truths and focus on individual existence, authenticity, and the “death of
God”.
 Edmund Husserl (1859–1938) developed Phenomenology by emphasising
conscious experience as the foundation of knowledge.

 Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980) and Martin Heidegger (1889–1976) expanded
Existentialism, arguing for radical human freedom and responsibility (Han, 2006).
E. Analytic Philosophy and 20th-Century Developments
 Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) argued that language shapes reality. He
divided his work into early logical positivism and later ordinary language
philosophy.
 Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) and G.E. Moore (1873–1958) critiqued Idealism,

 Postmodernism, led by Michel Foucault (1926–1984) and Jacques Derrida
(1930–2004), deconstructed truth, power, and language, challenging the
foundations of Western philosophy (Sevostyanov, 2023).
III. Contemporary Philosophy
Contemporary philosophy emerged in the late 19th and 20th centuries and has been shaped by
scientific advancements, globalisation, and interdisciplinary influences. It includes movements
such as Analytic Philosophy, Continental Philosophy, Postmodernism, and Political Philosophy,
all of which have engaged with modern social, ethical, and epistemological issues (Bartels,
Onstenk, & Veugelers, 2016). This period emphasised historicism, questioning whether
philosophy should evolve based on contemporary concerns rather than being tied to historical
traditions (Menzhulin, 2022).
A. Analytic Philosophy and Its Influence
Molokwu (2009) revealed that the analytic movement was brought by G. E. Moore and Bertrand
Russell. They opined that philosophy should concern itself with analysing language (Molokwu,

Existentialism, with figures like Jean-Paul Sartre and Martin Heidegger, focused on individual

psychoanalytic ideas to critique ideology and cultural systems.
C. Political and Ethical Philosophy
John Rawls revitalised political philosophy with his theory of justice which emphasised fairness
and social contract theory. Feminist and postcolonial philosophy, including bell hooks and Frantz
Fanon, critiqued traditional power structures and expanded the discourse on identity, race, and
gender (Xing, 2011).

3. Logic: The Science of Correct Thinking
 Definition and nature of logic
Man by nature is a logical being and without logic, man would cease to be man (Ukah, 2010).
This provides us with the need to ask questions like what is logic? Of what importance is logic to
man? To answer the question of what logic is, it can be noted that it has no universally accepted
definition as it has been defined in various ways by different scholars; however, the different
definitions point out some salient features of what logic is about (Ogbinaka, 2009). Ogbinaka
(2009) reveals that etymologically, logic is derived from the Greek word ‘Logos’ which means
‘word,’ ‘reason’ or ‘thought.’ Some definitions by scholars are given below:
Irving Copi sees logic as “the study of the methods and principles used to distinguish good
(correct) from bad (incorrect) reasonings” (Copi, 1978). Ogbinaka (2009) reveals that Copi
further gave two other definitions of logic: “Logic is the science of the law of thought” and
“logic is the science of reasoning,” which bring it closer to psychology.
A.R. Lacey opines that logic deals with “valid reasoning, its systematisation and the study of
notions relevant to it.” It has two main parts, formal logic and philosophical logic which is also
called logical theory (Lacey, n.d.). Moreover, Mourant defines “logic as both a science and an art,
concerned with the quest for knowledge and truth. It is also a study of the validity or correctness
of our reasoning” (Nze Agwaranze, 2005).
Leonard describes logic as “the science of exact reasoning.” He believes that the science of logic
seeks to discover and state laws in accordance with which any act of thought may be judge good
or bad (Nze Agwaranze, 2005). Bertrand Russell also defines logic in terms of the formal and
legitimate ways of reasoning validly (Russell, 1903).
Ogbinaka (2009) points out the following as important points to be gleaned from various
definitions of logic:
(i) Logic concerns orderly reasoning and thought (i.e thinking).
(ii) It is about inferences as it deals on how propositions are inferred.
(iii) It deals with the order and form of arguments.
(iv) It is about judgments and how these judgments are either arrived at or derived.
(v) It is more of a formal, not so much of factual inquiry into how we make our
judgments in reasoning.
(vi) Logic can only be the activity of rational human beings since it relates to thought.
The fundamental question of whether logic is a discovery about reality or a human construction
has been a long-standing philosophical debate. Logical realism, as advocated by thinkers like
A.N. Prior, asserts that logic is not merely a linguistic or conceptual construct but reflects an
objective structure of the world (Jakobsen, 2020). This suggests the idea that logical principles
govern reality independently of human cognition. Conversely, inferentialist and expressivist
views, such as those proposed by Brandom, argue that logic is shaped by human discourse and
the rules governing natural and artificial languages (Peregrin, 2019).

--- page 17 ---
Aristotle’s contribution remains one of the most enduring aspects of logic, with his syllogisms
establishing foundational principles for deductive reasoning (O’Regan, 2020). His work was
expanded by the Stoics, who introduced propositional logic, wherein logical statements could be
evaluated as true or false independent of their constituent terms. Later developments by thinkers
like Frege, Boole, and Russell revolutionised logic, transforming it into a formal system with
precise symbolic representation (O’Regan, 2020). Frege’s work was also important in shifting
logic from a language-dependent system to a formal, axiomatic structure (Shieh, 2019). His
conception of logic as a system of truths grounded in primitive axioms provided the basis for
modern predicate logic. He argued that logical truths justify themselves through an inherent
rational perception, which contrasts with later formalist interpretations that treat logic as an
abstract mathematical structure (Shieh, 2019).
In essence, logic, as a discipline, is concerned with the principles of valid inference, the nature of
truth, and the structure of rational argumentation. It reveals when, how and if reasoning,
judgement and arguments are correct or incorrect; valid or invalid.
 Types of logic: Formal and Informal logic
Logic is the foundation of structured reasoning as it provides a systematic method for evaluating
arguments and distinguishing valid inferences from fallacious ones. It is basically divided into
formal logic and informal logic.
 Formal Logic
Formal logic is the study of principles and structures that govern valid reasoning. It focuses on
the form of arguments rather than on their content and thus, provides a ground for distinguishing
between valid and invalid arguments. It also allows reasoning to be expressed in a precise and
systematic manner by using formal symbols and rules.
Historically, formal logic can be traced back to ancient philosophers such as Aristotle, who
formulated syllogistic logic to study deductive reasoning. His system was the first attempt to
establish a formal structure for logical arguments based on categorical propositions. In the 19th
and 20th centuries, logicians such as Frege, Peano, Russell, and Hilbert expanded upon
Aristotle’s ideas, developing symbolic logic and formal systems that laid the foundation for
modern logic (Ono, 2019). The formalisation of logic during this period led to breakthroughs in
the foundations of mathematics and the philosophy of logic.
Fundamentally, formal logic operates through well-defined syntactic rules and semantics that
determine how statements relate to one another. The syntax of formal logic consists of symbols
and rules for combining them into well-formed formulas and these formulas represent
propositions, which are declarative statements that can be either true or false. The semantics of
formal logic assigns meaning to these formulas by interpreting them within a logical system.
This dual structure—syntax and semantics—ensures that formal logic maintains both precision
and consistency (Polat, 2024).
Formal logic also relies on axioms and inference rules. Axioms are fundamental logical truths
assumed to be self-evident, while inference rules determine how new truths can be derived from

--- page 18 ---
existing ones. Logical systems such as propositional logic and predicate logic are built upon
these axioms and rules. Propositional logic, also known as sentential logic, deals with
propositions as whole units and focuses on logical connectives such as “and,” “or,” “if…then,”
and “not.” It evaluates the truth value of compound statements based on the truth values of their
components. Predicate logic, or first-order logic, extends propositional logic by incorporating
quantifiers (“for all” and “there exists”) and predicates, allowing for more complex reasoning
about objects and their properties (Polat, 2024).
The precision of formal logic makes it an essential tool in mathematical reasoning because it
provides the foundation for mathematical proofs by ensuring that conclusions follow necessarily
from premises. Mathematicians use formal logic to construct rigorous arguments, eliminating
ambiguity and potential errors in reasoning (Zach, 2024). The formalisation of mathematics has
also led to the development of mathematical logic, which deals with the relationship between
logic and mathematical structures. However, the application of formal logic extends beyond
mathematics and philosophy to incorporate fields such as computer science and artificial
intelligence. In computing, formal logic is used to design programming languages, verify
algorithms, and develop automated reasoning systems. Logic programming, a paradigm based on
formal logic, enables computers to perform logical inference and solve complex problems (Zach,
2024). Additionally, formal logic plays a vital role in artificial intelligence, specifically in
knowledge representation and automated theorem proving. As a result, AI systems can reason
about data and make decisions based on logical principles through encoding knowledge in a
logical format.
 Informal Logic
Informal logic is the study of reasoning as it naturally occurs in everyday language, focusing on
evaluating arguments in real-world contexts. Unlike formal logic, which relies on symbolic
notation and abstract structures to assess validity, informal logic is concerned with how
arguments are constructed, interpreted, and evaluated in practical discourse.
Basically, informal logic seeks to understand how people argue outside the rigid constraints of
formal systems. It recognises that in daily reasoning, arguments are often influenced by context,
emotions, rhetorical techniques, and audience perception (Tindale, 2019). Informal logic instead
asks whether an argument is reasonable and convincing given its context, rather than simply
whether it follows formal rules of inference. The rise of informal logic as a distinct field was a
response to the limitations of formal logic in evaluating everyday reasoning. Philosophers and
educators in the mid-20th century began to advocate for a more practical approach to logic that
can address reasoning in ordinary language rather than abstract symbols (Kakas, 2019).
One important component of informal logic is the analysis of argument structure. Informal logic
often deals with incomplete, implied, or context-dependent reasoning and thus, arguments are
evaluated based on their coherence, persuasiveness, and ability to withstand critical scrutiny.
This makes informal logic particularly useful in analysing persuasive communication, including
political speeches, advertising, legal arguments, and journalistic reports (Polat, 2024). A central
concern of informal logic is the identification of logical fallacies—flaws in reasoning that can

--- page 19 ---
make an argument misleading or unsound. Common informal fallacies include ad hominem
attacks (attacking the person rather than their argument), straw man arguments (misrepresenting
an opponent’s position to make it easier to refute), and false dilemmas (presenting only two
options when more exist (Gupta et al., 2021).
 Importance of Logic in Philosophy and Daily Life
Logic is important in both philosophy and daily life as it provides a structure for reasoning,
argumentation, and decision-making. It is the foundation of rational thought which enables
individuals to construct sound arguments, analyse complex problems and also distinguish
between valid and invalid reasoning.
In philosophy, logic is an essential tool used in evaluating arguments and looking into
fundamental questions about reality, knowledge and truth. Philosophers rely on logical principles
to assess the validity of arguments and avoid fallacies that may lead to incorrect conclusions.
Bertrand Russell described logic as “the essence of philosophy,” by highlighting that it clarifies
philosophical problems through rigorous reasoning (MacFarlane, 2020). Philosophical logic,
particularlly seeks to resolve ambiguities in natural language by analysing logical forms. This
method allows philosophers to construct precise arguments and addresses issues in metaphysics,
epistemology, ethics and the philosophy of language.
The stoics, including Epictetus, viewed logic as a necessary discipline for achieving wisdom and
virtue. They believed that logical reasoning protected the mind from fallacies, enabling
individuals to make rational decisions and distinguish between right and wrong [(Durhan, 2022).
This perspective emphasises the ethical dimension of logic, suggesting that clear reasoning
contributes to moral decision-making and intellectual freedom. Similarly, in modern times, logic
continues to play a role in ethical theories, legal reasoning, and debates about moral
responsibility.
Beyond philosophy, logic is indispensable in everyday life, influencing how individuals process
information, evaluate evidence, and make rational choices. Logical reasoning is crucial in
decision-making, allowing people to weigh different options, consider consequences, and reach
informed conclusions. Whether assessing financial investments, making healthcare choices, or
engaging in political discussions, logical thinking helps individuals navigate complex issues
without falling prey to misinformation or biases (Wong & Eurotech, 2021).
Logic also helps in providing clarity and coherence in discourse, thereby, enhancing
communication. It is also used in debates, journalism and everyday conversations to improve
persuasion and credibility. Moreover, one in recognising and avoiding fallacies which
strengthens critical thinking and promotes constructive dialogue (Okolo & Nwaorgu, 2021).
Furthermore, logic is important in science and technology as scientific inquiry depends on
logical reasoning to formulate hypotheses, conduct experiments and interpret data. Logical
principles help to ensure that scientific conclusions are based on sound reasoning rather than
fallacious assumptions (Zach, 2024).
