LESSON 1: UNDERSTANDING THE 
CRIME 
1 Introduction to cybercrime 
Computers, mobile devices, and the Internet have transformed how people communicate, 
learn, conduct business, store information, and access government services. 
However, the same technologies that create opportunities can also be abused. 
A person can use a computer to steal information, deceive another person, damage computer 
systems, distribute malicious software, commit financial fraud or interfere with important 
services. 
These unlawful activities are generally described as cybercrime. 
Cybercrime is therefore not simply "crime on the Internet." It covers a broad range of 
criminal activities involving computers, networks, digital devices, information systems or 
electronic data. 
2 Why should students study cybercrime? 
A Computer Science graduate may eventually work as: 
• A software developer 
• Network administrator 
• System administrator 
• Database administrator 
• Cybersecurity analyst 
• Digital forensic examiner 
• IT auditor 
• Security consultant 
• ICT manager 
• Incident response analyst 
• Academic or researcher 
In each of these professions, the individual may encounter cybercrime. 
For example, a system administrator may discover that an employee's account has been used 
to access confidential information. A cybersecurity analyst may detect suspicious network 

--- page 3 ---
traffic. A digital forensic examiner may be asked to examine a laptop involved in a criminal 
investigation. 
Therefore, students need both technical knowledge and legal awareness. 
 
3 Cybercrime as a technological and social 
problem 
Cybercrime should not be viewed only as a technical problem. 
It involves: 
Technology + People + Money + Law + Society 
For example, phishing involves technology, but the attack's success may depend on human 
trust. 
A criminal may create a convincing message claiming: 
"Your university account will be disabled unless you verify your account." 
The victim may click the link and unknowingly provide information. 
The technology facilitates the crime, but human behaviour contributes to its success. 
 
4 Basic characteristics of cybercrime 
Cybercrime often has the following characteristics: 
1. Borderless nature 
The offender may be in one country, the victim in another country, and the relevant server in 
a third country. 
2. Speed 
Digital information can be copied and transmitted extremely quickly. 
3. Anonymity 
Criminals may attempt to hide their identities using fake accounts, compromised systems or 
other techniques. 

--- page 4 ---
4. Scalability 
One malicious message can potentially reach thousands of people. 
5. Dependence on evidence 
Digital evidence can be fragile and can change quickly. 
6. Technical complexity 
Investigators may need specialist knowledge of computers, networks, cloud systems, 
databases and mobile devices. 
 
CLASSROOM DISCUSSION 
Ask students: 
Question: Is cybercrime a new type of crime or an old type of crime using new technology? 
Expected discussion: 
Many crimes existed before computers. Fraud, theft, impersonation, and extortion, for 
example, are not new. Technology has changed how these crimes can be committed, 
increased their speed, and expanded their geographical reach. 
 
LESSON 2: DEFINITION AND 
CONCEPT OF CYBERCRIME 
1 Definition 
Cybercrime can be defined generally as: 
Any unlawful activity in which a computer, computer system, network, digital device or 
digital information is used as a tool, target, environment or source of evidence. 
A computer is just the physical electronic device, while a computer system is the complete 
combination of that hardware, software, and users working together to perform tasks 
This definition is useful because computers can play different roles in crime. 
 

--- page 5 ---
2 Computer as the target 
Here, the computer system itself is attacked. 
Examples include: 
• Unauthorised access 
• Malware infection 
• Data destruction 
• Denial of service 
• System sabotage 
Example 
An attacker gains unauthorised access to a university server and deletes student records. 
The server is the target. 
 
3 Computer as the tool 
Here, the computer is used to commit another crime. 
Examples: 
Definitions of Cyber Terms 
• Online fraud: A crime where a person lies or hides facts online to get money or data 
from you, or uses any deceptive scheme using the Internet to trick victims out of 
money, property, or sensitive data. 
• Identity theft: Stealing someone's private personal information, like a name or 
account number, to pretend to be them for money or illegal acts. 
• Phishing: Sending fake emails or messages that look real to trick people into giving 
away passwords or bank details. 
• Cyberstalking: Using the internet, email, or social media to harass, track, or scare a 
specific person repeatedly. 
• Online impersonation: Pretending to be another real person online—often by 
creating fake social media profiles—to trick others or damage that person's reputation. 
Example 
A criminal uses email to impersonate a university bursar and deceive an employee into 
sending money to a fraudulent account. 
The computer is being used as a tool. 
 

--- page 6 ---
4 Computer as evidence 
Sometimes the computer itself is not the victim. 
Instead, it contains evidence. 
For example: 
A suspect may use a computer to communicate with another person. Investigators may later 
recover relevant emails, documents, messages, or transaction records. 
The computer becomes a source of evidence. 
 
5 Categories of cybercrime 
Cybercrime can be classified in several ways. 
1: Crimes against individuals 
Examples: 
• Identity theft 
• Cyberstalking 
• Online harassment 
• Phishing 
• Account compromise 
• Online impersonation 
2: Crimes against organisations 
Examples: 
• Corporate data theft 
• Ransomware 
• Insider attacks 
• Website compromise 
• Intellectual property theft 
3: Crimes against government 
Examples: 
• Attacks against government websites 
• Espionage

• Theft of government information 
• Disruption of government services 
4: Crimes against society 
Examples: 
• Large scale online fraud 
• Distribution of illegal content 
• Coordinated misinformation campaigns 
• Attacks affecting public services 
 
LESSON 3: TYPES OF CYBERCRIME 
1 Unauthorised access 
Unauthorised access occurs when a person accesses a computer system, account or network 
without lawful permission. 
Example 
A student obtains another student's password and logs into the student's university portal. 
Discussion 
Even if the student does not steal anything, unauthorised access may still be unlawful. 
 
2 Phishing 
Phishing involves attempts to deceive individuals into revealing sensitive information. 
Typical targets include: 
• Passwords 
• Banking information 
• Login credentials 
• Personal information 
Practical example 
A student receives: 

--- page 8 ---
"Your student portal account requires immediate verification." 
The message contains a fraudulent link. 
The student enters a username and password. 
The criminal receives the information. 
Prevention 
Students should: 
• Verify the sender 
• Avoid suspicious links 
• Check website addresses carefully 
• Use multifactor authentication 
• Report suspicious messages 
 
3 Identity theft 
Identity theft involves using another person's identifying information without lawful 
authority. 
Information may include: 
• Name 
• Identification details 
• Account information 
• Login credentials 
• Personal documents 
 
4 Online financial fraud 
This includes schemes designed to unlawfully obtain money through electronic 
communication or digital platforms. 
Examples include: 
• Fake investment schemes 
• Payment fraud 
• Business email compromise 
• Impersonation scams 
• Fraudulent online transactions 

--- page 9 ---
 
5 Malware 
Malware means malicious software. 
Examples include: 
• Viruses 
• Worms 
• Trojans 
• Ransomware 
• Spyware 
• Some forms of malicious scripts 
Ransomware 
Ransomware can prevent an organisation from accessing its files and demand payment. 
Classroom example 
Suppose a university department suddenly discovers that important administrative files 
cannot be opened. 
Students should ask: 
1. What happened? 
2. What evidence should be preserved? 
3. Should the affected computer immediately be reformatted? 
4. Who should be notified? 
5. What backups are available? 
 
6 Denial of Service 
A denial of service attack attempts to make a service unavailable to legitimate users. 
A distributed denial of service attack uses multiple systems or sources. 
Example 
A university website becomes unavailable during registration because it is receiving an 
abnormal volume of requests. 
 

--- page 10 ---
7 Cyberstalking and online harassment 
Technology can be misused to repeatedly intimidate, threaten or harass another person. 
Universities should have policies for reporting such incidents. 
 
8 Intellectual property crime 
Digital technology makes it easy to copy and distribute: 
• Software 
• Books 
• Videos 
• Research materials 
• Music 
• Databases 
Students should understand that easy copying does not automatically make copying lawful. 
 
9 Insider threats 
An insider threat occurs when someone with legitimate access misuses that access. 
Example: 
An employee with authorised access to student records copies confidential information and 
gives it to an unauthorised person. 
This demonstrates an important principle: 
Authorised access does not mean unlimited authority. 
 
LESSON 4: THREATS TO NATIONAL 
CRITICAL INFRASTRUCTURE 
1 Meaning of critical infrastructure 

--- page 11 ---
Critical infrastructure refers to systems and services that are essential to the functioning of a 
country or society. 
Examples can include: 
• Electricity 
• Telecommunications 
• Banking and financial services 
• Transportation 
• Healthcare 
• Government services 
• Water systems 
• Emergency services 
• Major information and communication systems 
 
2 Why critical infrastructure is attractive to 
attackers 
An attack against a critical service may affect many people simultaneously. 
For example: 
If a hospital's information system becomes unavailable, patient care may be affected. 
If a major financial system is disrupted, businesses and citizens may experience serious 
consequences. 
 
3 Types of threats 
Cyber espionage 
Unauthorised collection of sensitive information. 
Sabotage 
Intentional disruption or destruction of systems. 
Ransomware 
Malicious software that disrupts access to data or systems. 
Distributed denial of service 

--- page 12 ---
Attempts to overwhelm online services. 
Supply chain compromise 
An attacker compromises a supplier or third party to gain access to the final organisation.

LESSON 5: CYBERCRIME 
PERPETRATORS AND THEIR 
STRATEGIES 
1 Who commits cybercrime? 
Cybercrime perpetrators can include: 
• Individuals 
• Organised criminal groups 
• Disgruntled insiders 
• Fraud networks 
• Ideologically motivated groups 
• State associated actors 
• Opportunistic criminals 

--- page 13 ---
It is important not to assume that all cybercriminals have the same motivation. 
 
2 Common motivations 
Financial gain 
The offender wants money. 
Revenge 
The offender wants to harm an organisation or individual. 
Espionage 
The objective is to obtain information. 
Political or ideological objectives 
The offender wants to promote a particular cause. 
Recognition 
Some offenders may seek reputation within an online criminal community. 
 
3 Common social engineering strategies 
Cybercrime frequently exploits human psychology. 
Common techniques include: 
Urgency 
"You must act immediately." 
Fear 
"Your account will be closed." 
Authority 
"I am calling from the bank." 
Curiosity 

--- page 14 ---
"See the attached document." 
Reward 
"You have won a prize."
