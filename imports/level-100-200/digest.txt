[Normal] WEEK 2:
[Normal] Concept of Norms and Values; the acceptable norms and values of the major ethnic groups in Nigeria
[Normal] Concept of Norms and Values
[Normal] Norms and values are essential aspects of social life that guide behavior and interactions among individuals within a society. They form the foundation of culture, shaping what is considered acceptable or unacceptable within different communities.
[Normal] 1. Norms
[Normal] Norms are the established rules or expected behaviors in a society. They are informal guidelines that dictate how people should act in specific situations. Norms can be classified into two types:
[Normal] Folkways: These are everyday habits and customs that are not strictly enforced. For example, greeting elders in Nigeria with respect.
[Normal] Mores: These are strongly held norms with moral significance. Violating mores may result in serious consequences, such as public disgrace or legal punishment.
[Normal] 2. Values
[Normal] Values are the fundamental beliefs that guide a society’s perception of what is good, desirable, or appropriate. They influence norms and shape the collective conscience of a community. Examples of values include honesty, respect, hard work, and hospitality.
[Normal] In Nigerian society, norms and values are deeply embedded in the traditions of its various ethnic groups. Below are some of the acceptable norms and values of the major ethnic groups in Nigeria.
[Normal] Acceptable Norms and Values of Major Ethnic Groups in Nigeria
[Normal] Nigeria is home to over 250 ethnic groups, but the three major ones are the Hausa-Fulani, Yoruba, and Igbo. Each of these groups has unique cultural norms and values that govern their way of life.
[Normal] 1. Hausa-Fulani Norms and Values
[Normal] The Hausa-Fulani ethnic group, mainly found in northern Nigeria, has strong Islamic and traditional influences that shape their norms and values.
[Normal] Norms
[Normal] Respect for elders: Young people are expected to show deep respect for their elders through greetings and body language.
[Normal] Modesty in dressing: Both men and women dress conservatively, with women covering their bodies according to Islamic traditions.
[Normal] Politeness and humility: Individuals are expected to be polite in speech and behavior.
[Normal] Purdah system: In some parts of the north, married women live in seclusion and are expected to maintain modesty.
[Normal] Early marriage: It is common for girls to marry at a young age due to cultural and religious beliefs.
[Normal] Religious devotion: Islam plays a central role in daily life, influencing prayer, fasting, and social interactions.
[Normal] Values
[Normal] Faith (Iman): Religious devotion is a key value among the Hausa-Fulani.
[Normal] Discipline and self-control: Individuals are expected to live a disciplined life, following religious and societal rules.
[Normal] Hard work (Aikin yi): Farming and trading are highly valued professions.
[Normal] Hospitality: Visitors are treated with warmth and generosity.
[Normal] 2. Yoruba Norms and Values
[Normal] The Yoruba ethnic group, mainly found in southwestern Nigeria, has a rich culture emphasizing respect, community, and education.
[Normal] Norms
[Normal] Greeting with prostration or kneeling: Young men prostrate and young women kneel when greeting elders.
[Normal] Use of honorific titles: Elders and respected individuals are addressed with titles like "Baba," "Iya," or "Kabiyesi" for kings.
[Normal] Festival celebrations: Traditional festivals such as the Osun-Osogbo Festival are widely observed.
[Normal] Polygamy: While less common today, polygamous marriages are still culturally accepted.
[Normal] Respect for traditional rulers: Kings and chiefs, known as Obas, are highly revered.
[Normal] Values
[Normal] Education: The Yoruba emphasize formal education and intellectual excellence.
[Normal] Respect (Owo): Respect for elders and authority is a fundamental value.
[Normal] Hard work and entrepreneurship: Many Yoruba people engage in business and skilled professions.
[Normal] Religious tolerance: The Yoruba are known for embracing Islam, Christianity, and traditional religions peacefully.
[Normal] Extended family system: Family bonds are strong, and relatives play key roles in decision-making.
[Normal] 3. Igbo Norms and Values
[Normal] The Igbo ethnic group, primarily in southeastern Nigeria, is known for its emphasis on entrepreneurship, communal living, and respect for elders.
[Normal] Norms
[Normal] Age-grade system: Young people form age groups that contribute to community development.
[Normal] Respect for elders: Elders are highly respected, and younger people greet them with honor.
[Normal] Traditional marriage rites: Marriage ceremonies involve payment of the bride price and family introductions.
[Normal] Title-taking (Chieftaincy titles): Acquiring traditional titles such as "Ozo" or "Ichie" signifies wealth and social status.
[Normal] Respect for ancestors: Traditional practices include honoring ancestors through ceremonies and festivals.
[Normal] Values
[Normal] Hard work and self-reliance: The Igbo strongly believe in individual effort and self-determination.
[Normal] Entrepreneurship: Business and trade are highly valued, with many Igbo people engaging in commerce.
[Normal] Community service: Helping the community is seen as a responsibility.
[Normal] Education: Like the Yoruba, the Igbo place high importance on formal education.
[Normal] Courage and bravery: Igbo people admire individuals who show resilience and courage in facing challenges.
[Normal] Conclusion
[Normal] Norms and values play a crucial role in shaping Nigerian society. Despite cultural differences, common values such as respect, hard work, and communal living unite the various ethnic groups. Understanding these norms and values fosters social harmony and strengthens national unity.

[Normal] WEEK 3
[Normal] TOPIC: The Historical Foundation of Nigerian Culture and Arts in Pre-Colonial Times (People of Southern and Northern Nigeria)
[Normal] The Historical Foundation of Nigerian Culture and Arts in Pre-Colonial Times
[Normal] Nigerian culture and arts have deep historical roots dating back thousands of years before the colonial era. The cultural and artistic traditions of pre-colonial Nigeria were shaped by various ethnic groups, kingdoms, and empires that flourished in both the northern and southern regions of the country. These cultures produced remarkable artistic works, social structures, and traditions that influenced their way of life.
[Normal] 1. The People and Their Civilizations
[Normal] Nigeria is home to over 250 ethnic groups, but in pre-colonial times, two broad regions—northern and southern Nigeria—had distinct yet interconnected cultural and artistic traditions.
[Normal] A. The Peoples of Southern Nigeria
[Normal] The southern part of Nigeria was inhabited by groups such as the Yoruba, Igbo, Edo (Bini), Ijaw, Ibibio, and several others. These societies had well-established kingdoms, city-states, and cultural traditions.
[Normal] Yoruba Civilization (Ife, Oyo, and Ijebu Kingdoms):
The Yoruba people of southwestern Nigeria developed one of the most sophisticated artistic traditions in pre-colonial Africa. The ancient city of Ife, believed to be the spiritual center of the Yoruba, was renowned for its naturalistic terracotta and bronze sculptures. These artworks, dating back to the 12th-15th centuries, were highly detailed and showcased the advanced metallurgical skills of the Yoruba people. The Oyo Empire was also a dominant force, known for its military strength, political organization, and artistic expressions in textiles, beadwork, and wood carving.
[Normal] Benin Kingdom (Edo People):
The Benin Kingdom (modern-day Edo State) was one of the most advanced pre-colonial African states. It was famous for its highly detailed bronze and ivory carvings, which depicted historical events, rulers, and deities. The Benin Bronzes, which adorned the Oba's palace, were a testament to the kingdom’s artistic excellence and were made using the lost-wax casting technique.
[Normal] Igbo Societies (Nri Kingdom and Arochukwu Confederacy):
The Igbo people lived in decentralized communities, but some regions had influential centers like the Nri Kingdom, which emphasized peace, ritual kingship, and art. The Igbo-Ukwu civilization (9th-10th century) produced intricate bronze works that showed a mastery of metalwork. The Arochukwu Confederacy was another powerful political and religious center that played a major role in regional trade.
[Normal] B. The Peoples of Northern Nigeria
[Normal] Northern Nigeria was dominated by powerful Hausa city-states, the Kanem-Bornu Empire, and the Fulani people. These societies had well-structured political systems, Islamic influences, and distinct artistic traditions.
[Normal] Hausa City-States (Kano, Zaria, Katsina, Gobir, etc.):
The Hausa city-states, which emerged between the 10th and 15th centuries, were significant trade centers along the trans-Saharan trade routes. Their artistic expressions were evident in architecture (notably in Kano’s ancient city walls and mosques), leatherwork, metalwork, and textile production (such as the famous indigo-dyed Kano cloth).
[Normal] Kanem-Bornu Empire:
The Kanem-Bornu Empire (9th–19th century), ruled by the Sayfawa dynasty, had strong connections with North Africa and the Arab world. Its artistic traditions included Arabic calligraphy, mosque architecture, and military craftsmanship. The empire was known for its horse-mounted warriors, whose leather armor and weaponry demonstrated advanced metalworking skills.
[Normal] The Fulani and Islamic Influence:
The Fulani people, especially during the 19th-century Sokoto Caliphate led by Usman dan Fodio, introduced a strong Islamic influence in northern Nigeria. Calligraphy, mosque architecture, and Quranic manuscript illumination flourished in the region. The Fulani also contributed to Nigeria’s artistic heritage through their distinctive clothing, woven mats, and body art.
[Normal] 2. Artistic and Cultural Traditions
[Normal] A. Sculpture and Carving
[Normal] The Ife and Benin bronzes remain some of the most sophisticated artworks from pre-colonial Africa.
[Normal] The Nok Culture, one of the earliest known civilizations in Nigeria (900 BCE–200 CE), produced terracotta figurines that depicted humans and animals in stylized forms.
[Normal] Igbo-Ukwu bronze works showed detailed craftsmanship, suggesting that metal casting was an established practice in ancient Igbo societies.
[Normal] B. Textiles and Weaving
[Normal] The Hausa and Yoruba were renowned for textile production, using dyed fabrics and woven materials.
[Normal] The famous adire cloth of the Yoruba people was made using resist-dye techniques, creating intricate patterns on indigo-dyed fabrics.
[Normal] Hausa weavers produced elaborately embroidered robes, including the famous Babban Riga and Fula garments.
[Normal] C. Pottery and Ceramics
[Normal] The Gwari and Nupe people in the north, as well as the Igbo and Yoruba in the south, were known for pottery-making, producing clay pots, decorative vessels, and ritual objects.
[Normal] D. Music and Dance
[Normal] Yoruba talking drums (Dundun) were an essential part of social and religious ceremonies.
[Normal] Igbo and Efik societies used slit drums and gongs in traditional governance and rituals.
[Normal] The Hausa and Kanuri people had a rich tradition of using stringed instruments like the Goje (a type of lute) and percussion instruments for storytelling and praise-singing.
[Normal] E. Architecture
[Normal] In northern Nigeria, Sudanese and Sahelian architectural styles influenced palace and mosque designs, as seen in the Gobarau Minaret of Katsina and the ancient city walls of Kano.
[Normal] In southern Nigeria, traditional palaces and shrines, such as the Ooni’s Palace in Ife and the Oba’s Palace in Benin, were adorned with elaborate carvings and relief sculptures.
[Normal] 3. Socio-Political and Religious Influence on Culture and Arts
[Normal] Traditional Religion and Art:
Art was deeply connected to spirituality. Many pre-colonial Nigerian societies created masks, sculptures, and ritual objects used in religious ceremonies, such as the Yoruba Orisha worship and the Igbo Mbari houses dedicated to the gods.
[Normal] Islamic Influence:
Northern Nigeria’s artistic heritage was shaped by Islamic calligraphy, geometric designs, and mosque architecture. The spread of Islam also influenced clothing styles and literary traditions.
[Normal] Trade and Cultural Exchange:
The trans-Saharan trade routes facilitated the exchange of artistic techniques, materials, and styles between northern Nigeria and North Africa, while coastal trade introduced new influences to southern Nigeria.
[Normal] Conclusion
[Normal] The historical foundation of Nigerian culture and arts in pre-colonial times reflects the creativity, sophistication, and diversity of the country’s various ethnic groups. The traditions of sculpture, textiles, music, architecture, and religious artistry not only served aesthetic purposes but also played crucial roles in governance, spirituality, and social organization. These artistic legacies continue to influence modern Nigerian culture, preserving the rich heritage of the past.

1. Fundamentals of Computer Design
1.1 Introduction
The concept of stored program computers appeared in 1945 when John von
Neumann drafted the ﬁrst version of EDV AC (Electronic Discrete Variable
Computer). Those ideas have since been the milestones of computers:
• an input device through which data and instructions can be
entered
• storage in which data can be read/written; instructions are like
data, they reside in the same memory
• an arithmetic unit to process data
• a control unit which fetches instructions, decode and execute them
• output devices for the user to access the results.
The improvements in computer technology have been tremendous since
the ﬁrst machines appeared. A personal computer that can be bought today
with a few thousand dollars, has more performance (in terms of, say,
ﬂoating point multiplications per second), more main memory and more
disk capacity than a machine that cost millions in the 50s-60s.

Four lines of evolution have emerged from the ﬁrst computers (deﬁnitions
are very loose and in many case the borders between different classes are
blurring):
1. Mainframes: large computers that can support very many users
while delivering great computing power. It is mainly in
mainframes where most of the innovations (both in architecture
and in organization) have been made.
2. Minicomputers:   have    adopted     many    of    the   mainframe
techniques, yet being designed to sell for less, satisfying the
computing needs for smaller groups of users. It is the
minicomputer group that improved at the fastest pace (since 1965
when DEC introduced the ﬁrst minicomputer, PDP-8), mainly due
to the evolution of integrated circuits technology (the ﬁrst IC
appeared in 1958).
3. Supercomputers: designed for scientiﬁc applications, they are
the most expensive computers (over one million dollars),
processing is usually done in batch mode, for reasons of
performance.
4. Microcomputers : have appeared in the microprocessor era
(the    ﬁrst microprocessor, Intel 4004, was introduced in 1971).
The term  micro  refers only to physical dimensions, not to
computing performance. A typical microcomputer (either a PC or
a workstation) nicely ﬁts on a desk. Microcomputers are a direct
product of technological advances: faster CPUs, semiconductor
memories, etc. Over the time many of the concepts previously
used in mainframes and minicomputers have become common
place in microcomputers.
For many years the evolution of computers was concerned with the
problem of object code compatibility. A new architecture had to be, at least
partly, compatible with older ones. Older programs (“the dusty deck”) had
to run without changes on the new machines. A dramatic example is the
IBM-PC architecture, launched in 1981, it proved so successful that further
developments had to conform with the ﬁrst release, despite the ﬂaws which
became apparent in a couple of years thereafter.
The assembly language is no longer the language in which new
applications are written, although the most sensitive parts continue to be
written in assembly language, and this is due to advances in languages and
compiler technology.

The obsolescence of assembly language programming, as well as the

1.2 Performance Deﬁnition
What is the meaning of saying that a computer is faster than another one? It
depends upon the position you have: if you are a simple user (end user)
then you say a computer is faster when it runs your program in less time,
and you think at the time it takes from the moment you launch your
program until you get the results, this the so called wall-clock time. On the
other hand, if you are system's manager, then you say a computer is faster
when it completes more jobs per time unit.
As a user you are interested in reducing the  response time (also called the
execution time or latency). The computer manager is more interested in
increasing the  throughput (also called  bandwidth ), the number of jobs
done in a certain amount of time.
Response time, execution time and throughput are usually connected to
tasks and whole computational events. Latency and bandwidth are mostly
used when discussing about memory performance.

Propositional Logic
A field of mathematics known as propositional logic examines the logical connections between
propositions—also known as statements,  sentences, or assertions—when viewed as a whole and
joined by logical connectives.

Philosophy is derived from the Greek words philo (love) and sophia (wisdom), meaning "love of

reasoning and inquiry.
 Systematic Inquiry: It employs structured methods to explore fundamental issues.

human thought and existence:

questions like: What can we know? How do we acquire knowledge? What distinguishes

aesthetically pleasing and how artistic expression impacts human experience.

[Normal] What is Structured Analysis?
[Normal] Structured analysis is a software engineering methodology that utilizes graphical representation to convert real-world cases into data specifications that can be used to create computer programs and hardware configurations. It is an important process-oriented approach because it allows a logical step-wise breakdown of both the source of information and the target of what is to be developed.
[Normal] A software engineer utilizes a data flow diagram which is a set of bubbles with arrows connected to the direction of flow of data. A top-down approach is used to decompose and solve specific problems. This methodology of structured analysis was developed to document system requirements and designs.
[Normal] History of Structured Analysis
[Normal] The 1960s to the 1980s saw a large demand for complex information systems. At that time, there were no standard techniques for documenting requirements, configurations, and processes involved in a software system.
[Normal] What are structured analysis techniques?
[Normal] Structured analysis techniques are of three types:
[Normal] Functional — This technique utilizes data flow diagrams to depict functions and flow of data.
[Normal] Data — This technique utilizes entity relationship diagrams to depict the dependencies of the software system with the external environment.
[Normal] Dynamic — This technique utilizes state transition diagrams to depict the conditions and working of the system.
[Normal] What is the main aim of structured analysis?
[Normal] The main aim of structured analysis is to break down complex information into smaller simpler parts so as to aid in the efficient development of a software system. The main characteristic of structured analysis is the usage of diagrams to display various functions and the flow of data.
[Normal] What is a data flow diagram?
[Normal] A data flow diagram (DFD) maps out the flow of information for any process or system. It uses defined symbols like rectangles, circles and arrows, plus short text labels, to show data inputs, outputs, storage points and the routes between each destination.
[Normal] They can be used to analyse an existing system or model a new one.
[Normal] a DFD can often visually “say” things that would be hard to explain in words, and they work for both technical and nontechnical audiences, from developer to CEO.
[Normal] Data-flow diagrams provide a graphical representation of the system that aims to be accessible to
[Normal]  computer specialist and non-specialist users alike. The models enable software engineers, customers
[Normal]  and users to work together effectively during the analysis and specification of requirements.
[Normal] Symbols and Notations Used in DFDs
[Normal] Two common systems of symbols are named after their creators:
[List Paragraph] Yourdon and Coad /Yourdon and DeMarco
[List Paragraph] Gane and Sarson
[Normal] One main difference in their symbols is that Yourdon-Coad and Yourdon-DeMarco use circles for processes, while Gane and Sarson use rectangles with rounded corners, sometimes called lozenges. There are other symbol variations in use as well, so the important thing to keep in mind is to be clear and consistent in the shapes and notations you use to communicate and collaborate with others.
[Normal] Using any convention’s DFD rules or guidelines, the symbols depict the four components of data flow diagrams.
[Normal] External entity: an outside system that sends or receives data, communicating with the system being diagrammed. They are the sources and destinations of information entering or leaving the system. They might be an outside organization or person, a computer system or a business system. They are also known as terminators, sources and sinks or actors. They are typically drawn on the edges of the diagram.
[Normal] Process: any process that changes the data, producing an output. It might perform computations, or sort data based on logic, or direct the data flow based on business rules. A short label is used to describe the process, such as “Submit payment.”
[Normal] Data store: files or repositories that hold information for later use, such as a database table or a membership form. Each data store receives a simple label, such as “Orders.”
[Normal] Data flow: the route that data takes between the external entities, processes and data stores. It portrays the interface between the other components and is shown with arrows, typically labelled with a short data name, like “Billing details.”
[Normal] A data flow diagram can dive into progressively more detail by using levels and layers, zeroing in on a particular piece.  DFD levels are numbered 0, 1 or 2, and occasionally go to even Level 3 or beyond. The necessary level of detail depends on the scope of what you are trying to accomplish.
[Normal] DFD Level 0 is also called a Context Diagram. It’s a basic overview of the whole system or process being analysed or modelled. It’s designed to be an at-a-glance view, showing the system as a single high-level process, with its relationship to external entities. It should be easily understood by a wide audience, including stakeholders, business analysts, data analysts and developers.
[Normal] DFD Level 1 provides a more detailed breakout of pieces of the Context Level Diagram. You will highlight the main functions carried out by the system, as you break down the high-level process of the Context Diagram into its subprocesses.
[Normal] DFD Level 2 then goes one step deeper into parts of Level 1. It may require more text to reach the necessary level of detail about the system’s functioning.
[Normal] Progression to Levels 3, 4 and beyond is possible, but going beyond Level 3 is uncommon. Doing so can create complexity that makes it difficult to communicate, compare or model effectively.
[Normal] Logical DFD vs. Physical DFD
[Normal] These are the two categories of a data flow diagram.  A Logical DFD visualizes the data flow that is essential for a business to operate. It focuses on the business and the information needed, not on how the system works or is proposed to work. However, a Physical DFD shows how the system is actually implemented now, or how it will be. For example, in a Logical DFD, the processes would be business activities, while in a Physical DFD, the processes would be programs and manual procedures.
[Normal] Logical and Physical Data Flow Diagrams (DFDs) are two different ways of representing a system’s data flow, each with its specific focus and purpose.
[Normal] 1. Logical DFD
[Normal] Purpose: A logical DFD focuses on what the system does, representing the business functions, processes, and information flow without going into technical details about how they are implemented.
[Normal] Components: It emphasizes the flow of data, the inputs and outputs of processes, and the overall system's functions.
[Normal] Details: Logical DFDs are often used during the initial stages of system design to understand the requirements and outline the functional aspects of the system. They help stakeholders and analysts get a clear picture of the system's purpose, requirements, and essential operations.
[Normal] Example: In a library system, a logical DFD would show processes like "Check Out Book" or "Return Book" and how they interact with data entities such as "Member Records" or "Book Inventory" without detailing the specific hardware or software involved.
[Normal] 2. Physical DFD
[Normal] Purpose: A physical DFD describes how the system will be implemented, specifying the actual hardware, software, files, databases, and people involved in the system’s operations.
[Normal] Components: It includes specific details about data storage locations, actual file formats, hardware requirements, and software applications used.
[Normal] Details: Physical DFDs are typically used in later stages of system development when focusing on implementation details. They help developers and system architects understand the exact setup and components needed to operate the system.
[Normal] Example: In a library system, a physical DFD would detail the specific database (e.g., SQL database) where "Member Records" are stored, the exact server or hardware used, and software tools like scanners or kiosks for "Check Out Book" processes.
[Normal] Key Differences
[Normal] Abstraction Level: Logical DFDs are more abstract and focus on business processes, while Physical DFDs are detailed and focus on technical aspects.
[Normal] Audience: Logical DFDs are useful for analysts and stakeholders to understand the system conceptually, whereas Physical DFDs are geared toward developers and IT professionals for implementation.
[Normal] Timing in Development: Logical DFDs are used in the early stages of system design, while Physical DFDs are used when moving towards implementation.
